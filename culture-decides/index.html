Ginés Sánchez

Culture Decides

Chapter one, in working notes · September 1, 2026

Organisations like to explain themselves with strategy. But strategy is usually the story told afterwards. Before strategy there is a loop, and the loop is cultural.

It runs like this. Someone speaks up, signs a form, sends a measurement, files a complaint. Someone else decides what that means. The institution makes a decision. Something material happens: money moves, a permit is issued, a shipment leaves, a hillside is cleared. The consequences land on people and places. And some account of those consequences—honest or flattering, complete or convenient—feeds the next decision.

Culture is how a group runs that loop. Which signals count as real. Whose account is believed without a fight. What gets written down, and what everyone knows but nobody records. What can be said in the meeting, and what can only be said in the parking lot.

That is why culture decides. Not in the soft sense that people matter. In the hard sense that by the time an organisation “makes a decision,” most of it has already been made—by what the organisation was able to perceive, willing to remember, and prepared to say out loud.

Seen this way, the familiar failures stop being mysteries.

Corruption is a private edit to the loop. Someone quietly controls what the institution can see or remember, and the decision comes out serving them. Corruption thrives when truth is expensive to recover.

Extractivism is the severed loop. The benefit flows to the actor, while the consequences land on someone—a worker, a town, a river—who cannot affect the next decision. Extractivism thrives when consequences are expensive to return to the decision-maker.

And forgetting is the erased loop. When an institution loses the record of what it perceived, whose account counted, what it decided and what followed, it does not become innocent. It becomes easy to fool—including by itself.

The hopeful part is that the loop can be engineered. When truth is cheap to recover, decisions are cheap to explain, and consequences come back fast, good work gets easier and corruption gets expensive. Culture still decides—it just decides with better information.

That is the argument I want to test, chapter by chapter: in companies, in ministries, in supply chains, and eventually in how organisations treat the living systems they depend on.

These are working notes, published early on purpose. If the argument is wrong somewhere, I would rather find out here than in chapter nine.
